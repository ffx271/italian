\part{Foundations} \label{M1-Foundations}
\chapter{Pronunciation \& the Alphabet} \label{M1-L1}

Italian is almost perfectly phonetic. Once you know the sounds, you
can read almost any word correctly.

\section{Key Sounds}\label{key-sounds}

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{l l l}
C + a/o/u & Hard K         & \iti{casa, cosa, cucina} \\
C + e/i   & Soft CH        & \iti{cena, ciao} \\
G + a/o/u & Hard G         & \iti{gatto, gomma} \\
G + e/i   & Soft J         & \iti{gelato, giorno} \\
GL        & Like \iti{lli} in ``million'' & \iti{gli} \\
GN        & Like \iti{ny} in ``canyon'' & \iti{gnocchi, bagno} \\
H         & Always silent  & \iti{ho, hanno} \\
R         & Rolled/trilled & \iti{burro} \\
Z         & TS or DZ       & \iti{zero, zia} \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Double consonants} are held longer. This changes meaning:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \iti{pala} (shovel) vs \iti{palla} (ball)
    \item \iti{nono} (ninth) vs \iti{nonno} (grandfather)
\end{itemize}

\section{Vowels}\label{vowels}

Always pure and consistent:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{A} = ``ah''
  \item \textbf{E} = ``eh''
  \item \textbf{I} = ``ee''
  \item \textbf{O} = ``oh''
  \item \textbf{U} = ``oo''
\end{itemize}

\section{Stress}\label{stress}

Most words stressed on the \textbf{second-to-last syllable}:
\iti{ra-GAZ-zo, bel-LIS-si-mo}. Written accent when stress falls on last
syllable: \iti{città, caffè}.


\chapter{Nouns and Grammatical Gender}\label{M1-L2}

Every Italian noun is \textbf{masculine} or \textbf{feminine}. There is no neutral ``it''.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{r l l}
        \itb{-o} & Usually masculine & \itb{-i}: \iti{libro → libri} \\
        \itb{-a} & Usually feminine  & \itb{-e}: \iti{casa → case} \\
        \itb{-e} & Either: learn individually & \itb{-i}: \iti{fiore → fiori, notte → notti} \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Always learn a noun with its article} as there are exceptions:
\iti{il problema} (masculine despite -a ending), \iti{la mano} (feminine despite -o ending).


\chapter{Definite and Indefinite Articles}\label{M1-L3}

Articles change depending on \textbf{gender} and the \textbf{sound} the noun starts with.

\section{Definite Articles: ``the''}\label{definite-articles-the}

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ r | c c }
        Singular (consonant)          & \iti{il}  & \iti{la} \\
        Singular (s+cons, z, gn, ps)  & \iti{lo}  & - \\
        Singular (vowel)              & \iti{l'}  & \iti{l'} \\
        Plural (regular)              & \iti{i}   & \iti{le} \\
        Plural (s+cons, z, gn, vowel) & \iti{gli} & - \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}


\section{Indefinite Articles: ``a/an''}\label{indefinite-articles-aan}


\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{ r | c c }
        Before consonant     & \iti{un}  & \iti{una} \\
        Before s+cons, z, gn & \iti{uno} & - \\
        Before vowel         & \iti{un}  & \iti{un'} \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{The four \iti{gli} triggers (masculine plural only)}\label{the-four-gli-triggers-masculine-plural-only}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{S + consonant}: \iti{gli studenti, gli scontrini}
    \item \textbf{Z}: \iti{gli zii, gli zaini}
    \item \textbf{GN}: \iti{gli gnocchi}
    \item \textbf{Vowel}: \iti{gli uomini, gli amici}
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Subject Pronouns}\label{M1-L4}
\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{r | l }
        \iti{io}   & I \\
        \iti{tu}   & you (singular, informal) \\
        \iti{lui / lei / Lei} & he / she / you (formal) \\
        \iti{noi}  & we \\
        \iti{voi}  & you (plural) \\
        \iti{loro} & they \\
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Italian usually drops the subject pronoun. The verb ending makes it clear:
\iti{Parlo italiano.} I speak Italian. (\iti{Io parlo} is less natural.)
